Japanese food can look easy for restricted diets: rice, tofu, vegetables and seafood are at its centre. In practice, fish stock, alcohol-based seasonings and animal fats appear in many dishes where you would not expect them. This article covers what to watch for, how to explain your needs, and the official allergen labelling rules.

## The hidden ingredients

The main issue for vegetarians and vegans is dashi, a stock usually made with fish. JNTO's vegetarian and vegan guide notes that even stocks and sauces usually contain some meat-based ingredients, and that hidden ingredients such as meat or bonito flakes often make a dish unsuitable.

Examples JNTO gives:

- **Natto** packs may come with a sauce that is typically dashi.
- **Onigiri** (rice balls) from convenience stores often contain fish. Umeboshi (pickled plum) and kombu (kelp) fillings are the usual alternatives.
- **Shojin ryori**, Buddhist temple cuisine, is traditionally vegan and avoids garlic and onion, but it may include dairy, so check first.
- **Okonomiyaki** can be made vegetarian, but it is not vegan because it contains egg.

For Muslim travellers, JNTO's guide points to different risks. Cooking sake and mirin (a sweet cooking sake) are used in many Japanese dishes. Some miso contains alcohol unless it is additive-free and labelled "mu-ten-ka". Gelatine and animal fats such as butter may be present in breads, snacks and sweets. Alcohol is served in most Japanese restaurants.

## Explaining what you can and cannot eat

JNTO's advice for vegetarians and vegans is to be specific. Saying "vegetarian" alone may not be enough, because the word is understood more loosely in Japan. State that you eat no meat, seafood or animal products, including dashi and stocks. If staff seem unsure, name chicken, pork and fish as well.

Practical steps from the same guide:

- Carry a written card in Japanese listing your dietary requirements.
- Check photo menus for base ingredients, but remember dashi is not visible.
- Tell restaurants in advance, especially for reservations and set menus.
- Use a translation app when you need to explain something in more detail.

For allergies, the Consumer Affairs Agency (CAA) publishes a free **Food Allergy Communication Sheet** for smartphones in English, Chinese, Korean and Japanese. It is listed on the CAA's English food labelling page under "For Consumers". The CAA notes that the sheets should be downloaded before use, as the check boxes do not work otherwise, and recommends Adobe Reader.

## Vegetarian and vegan options and resources

JNTO's guide lists dishes that are often suitable, with the usual caution about stocks and sauces: vegetable tempura, tsukemono (pickles), tofu, nasu dengaku (grilled aubergine with miso), zaru soba, kappa maki (cucumber rolls) and takuan maki (pickled radish rolls). Convenience-store options it mentions include edamame, inarizushi, and umeboshi or kombu onigiri.

To find restaurants, JNTO names Happy Cow, Vegewel, Japan VegeMap and a Japan Vegetarian Society restaurant guide (in Japanese only). Many vegetarian and vegan restaurants display a sticker of approval from The Japan Vegan Society, VegeProject Japan or The Japan Vegetarian Association.

## Halal food and prayer spaces

JNTO states that there is no central agency for halal accreditation in Japan. It also warns that restaurants describing themselves as halal-friendly may not prepare food in a separate halal-only kitchen, so ask if this matters to you. The average supermarket does not stock halal products; halal-certified groceries are mostly frozen meat and spices.

On prayer, JNTO says prayer rooms and mosques are mostly found in larger cities, and that major airports, tourist sites and Muslim-friendly hotels increasingly offer places to pray. Some hotels provide prayer mats and an indication of the direction of Mecca.

JNTO runs a **Muslim Guide** section on its website with trip-planning articles and links to external sites for finding prayer rooms and mosques. Its Muslim travellers page also lists independent resources, including Halal Gourmet Japan, Halal Media Japan and HalalJapan.jp. JNTO suggests that rental apartments or rooms with a kitchenette make it easier to prepare your own halal meals.

## Allergy labelling in Japan

Under Japan's food labelling rules, it is mandatory to show the presence of certain allergens in foods and additives. The CAA's allergen labelling summary divides them into two groups.

| Group | Items | Labelling |
|---|---|---|
| Specified ingredients | Shrimp, crab, walnut, wheat, buckwheat, egg, dairy products, peanut | Mandatory |
| Items equivalent to specified ingredients | Almond, abalone, squid, salmon roe, orange, cashew nut, kiwi fruit, beef, sesame, salmon, mackerel, soybean, chicken, banana, pork, macadamia nut, peach, yam, apple, gelatine | Recommended |

Two points follow from this. First, the eight mandatory items must be shown, but the other 20 are only recommended, so their absence from a label does not prove a food is free of them. Second, sesame, soybean, almond, cashew nut and macadamia nut are in the recommended group, not the mandatory one. Foods not on either list are not covered by these rules. The CAA states that, as a general rule, allergens must be listed individually in the ingredients, but they may be listed collectively in exceptional cases.

The CAA list concerns food labelling. In restaurants and at food stalls, ask staff directly and show your communication sheet or card.

## Ryokan meals

Ryokan dinners are often kaiseki, a set sequence of small seasonal courses. Tell the inn about dietary needs and allergies when you book, not on arrival. JNTO's vegetarian guide also advises contacting a ryokan ahead of time so it can prepare vegetarian meals. See [Ryokan and onsen: the basics](/guide/ryokan-and-onsen).

## Before you go

- Prepare a written card in Japanese and download the CAA Food Allergy Communication Sheet if you have allergies.
- Mention dashi by name if you do not eat fish.
- Notify ryokan and restaurants with set menus when you book.
- Check labels for the eight mandatory allergens, but do not rely on labels alone for items in the recommended group.
- Halal-certified food is easiest to find through guides written for Muslim visitors; JNTO lists several.
