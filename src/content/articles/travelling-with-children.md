Japan's railways set clear fare rules for children, and the main long-distance trains have some facilities for babies. This article covers JR's age bands and when young children ride free, the child Japan Rail Pass, taking a stroller on board, and what JNTO says about nursing rooms and family travel.

## JR fare age bands

JR Central publishes the age bands used for JR fares. They are:

| Category | Age |
|---|---|
| Adult | 12 years old or older |
| Child | 6 to 11 years old |
| Infant | 1 to 5 years old |
| Baby | younger than 1 year old |

There is one exception to the age rule. JR Central says a 12-year-old who is still at elementary school counts as a child. This applies to elementary schools established under Japanese law.

Child basic fares, and child limited express, express and reserved seat tickets, cost half the adult price, according to JR Central.

## When infants and babies ride free

JR Central says infants and babies "have no charges" except in three situations:

- when more than two infants travel with one adult or child. Fares and charges are needed for the third and any further infants
- when an infant or baby uses a reserved seat, a Green Car seat (other than an unreserved Green Car seat), or a bed
- when an infant travels alone

Under these rules, one adult travelling with one or two children aged under 6 who do not use a reserved seat, Green Car seat or bed pays nothing extra for them. If you want a reserved seat of their own for a child under 6, a fare and charges apply.

JNTO's family travel tips give the same general picture. They say children aged 12 and over pay the adult fare, those aged 6 to 11 get a discount, and "usually, toddlers and infants (5 years old and under) travel free."

## The child Japan Rail Pass

The official Japan Rail Pass site defines "children" as those aged 6 to 11 at the time of use. For online purchases, a child who is 11 on the payment date is treated as a child. For passes bought overseas, a child who is 11 on the date the exchange voucher is issued is still treated as a child.

The official site lists these child prices:

| Pass | Ordinary, online | Green, online | Ordinary, overseas | Green, overseas |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 7 days | ¥25,000 | ¥35,000 | ¥27,000 | ¥37,000 |
| 14 days | ¥40,000 | ¥55,000 | ¥42,000 | ¥58,000 |
| 21 days | ¥50,000 | ¥70,000 | ¥53,000 | ¥74,000 |

The Japan Rail Pass pages we checked do not set out separate rules for children under 6. Check the general JR fare rules above, and ask at a JR ticket office if you want a reserved seat for a child of that age. For whether the pass is worth buying at all, see [Is the JR Pass still worth it?](/questions/is-the-jr-pass-still-worth-it) and [Rail and the JR Pass](/guide/rail-and-the-jr-pass).

## Strollers on trains and the Shinkansen

On the Tokaido, Sanyo, Kyushu and Nishi-Kyushu Shinkansen, JR Central says the oversized baggage rules "do not apply to strollers". You do not need a reservation to bring one on board. A stroller can go in the overhead racks or in the baggage storage on the deck between cars. If you want to use an oversized baggage area or compartment, such as the space behind the last row of seats, you need a reservation for it.

On ordinary trains, JNTO says some carriages are designated as stroller- and wheelchair-friendly, with extra space for prams. Look for carriage numbers and stroller pictograms on the platform markings. JNTO also advises folding the pram and using a baby carrier on crowded trains, and avoiding trains at busy hours where you can. It notes that most major stations have elevators.

JR West's manners guidance asks passengers not to leave suitcases and other large items around the train doors, so that others can get on and off easily. The same applies to a folded stroller.

## Baby facilities on board and at stations

On the Tokaido-Sanyo Shinkansen, JR West says the toilets in all cars are Western style and include a diaper changing table. It also says "a multipurpose room is available for various purposes such as nursing a baby or getting changed", and that customers with disabilities should be given priority use of these rooms. JR Central describes the multi-purpose room on its trains as being "primarily for disabled passengers".

JNTO's family tips say the multi-purpose rooms on long-distance trains "are usually locked, so you may have to ask the train staff for assistance."

Away from the trains, JNTO says nursing rooms are found in department stores, stations and malls. It says many have microwaves for baby food, private nursing cubicles and changing mats. It also says baby food is easy to find at most convenience stores.

JR West's manners page notes that priority seats are for expectant mothers, nursing mothers, the elderly, people with disabilities and others who need them.

## Planning notes

- **Accommodation.** JNTO says Western-style beds or travel cots are not always available at a ryokan, so check bedding before you book. Many ryokan use futons on tatami for the whole family. See [Ryokan and onsen](/guide/ryokan-and-onsen).
- **Strollers and carriers.** JNTO notes that parents in Japan often switch between a stroller and a baby carrier depending on the day. A carrier may be easier in stations or areas without lifts and ramps.
- **Stroller rental.** JNTO says many major sightseeing spots, such as theme parks, museums and airports, offer free or low-cost stroller rental.
- **Restaurants.** JNTO says restaurants tend to welcome children, and that "fami-resu" (family restaurants) are designed with children in mind.
- **Driving.** JNTO says rental car companies often offer child seats as an optional extra.
- **Holiday periods.** For Japan's main national holiday periods, see [Peak holiday periods](/articles/peak-holiday-periods).
