The Shikoku pilgrimage, or henro, is a circuit of 88 Buddhist temples around the island of Shikoku. This article covers what the pilgrimage is, how long it is, the dress and stamp book, the etiquette at each temple, and the choice between walking and travelling by car or bus. It uses the temple association, the regional tourism body and Japan's tourism authorities as sources.

## What the pilgrimage is

The Shikoku 88 Sacred Sites Association describes the pilgrimage as "an act of journeying through the entire Shikoku area based on the 88 sacred sites in Shikoku". It is linked to Kobo Daishi (Kukai). The association says he was born in 774 in what is now Zentsuji City, Kagawa Prefecture, founded Shingon Buddhism, and is associated with Koyasan. It attributes the selection of the 88 sites to him.

The Japan Tourism Agency's explanation says anyone may take part, regardless of religious belief, if they follow basic guidelines and approach it with respect. It also notes that local people have long offered pilgrims food, drink, small gifts or lodging. The Shikoku Henro World Heritage Inscription Council, whose office is in the Kagawa Prefectural Government, calls this custom settai and says it continues today. The council says the pilgrimage was certified as Japan Heritage by the Agency for Cultural Affairs in 2015.

## Length and the four prefectures

The temples are spread across Shikoku's four prefectures: Tokushima, Kochi, Ehime and Kagawa. The World Heritage council names a theme for each:

| Prefecture | Theme (council's English) |
|---|---|
| Tokushima | Path of Awakening |
| Kochi | Path of Discipline |
| Ehime | Path of Enlightenment |
| Kagawa | Path of Nirvana |

Official sources give different lengths. The Japan Tourism Agency says walking the full route "adds up to a journey of more than 1,400 kilometers", and the World Heritage council gives approximately 1,400 km. The Shikoku Tourism Creation Organization and JNTO both give about 1,200 km. Treat the figure as approximate.

Many pilgrims start at Temple 1, Ryozenji, in Tokushima and finish at Temple 88, Okuboji, in Kagawa, according to the Shikoku tourism body. The temple association says it is not necessary to visit in order: pilgrims choose where to start. It also describes a reverse-order practice, from Temple 88 to Temple 1. A final visit to Koyasan is optional, according to the association's FAQ. See [Koyasan temple stays](/articles/koyasan-temple-stays).

## Walking, car or bus

The temple association says pilgrims can walk, drive or use public transport, and can combine them. The Shikoku tourism body gives these estimates for a full circuit:

| Method | Time | Cost (tourism body's estimate) |
|---|---|---|
| Walking | about 6 weeks, at an average of 30 km a day | about 400,000 yen |
| Bus tour | 9 to 12 days | about 250,000 yen |
| Car | about 10 days | about 140,000 yen |

The same page says bus tours include an official guide (sendatsu), but no foreign-language bus tours are currently offered. JNTO's page gives similar estimates: about six weeks on foot, and nine to 12 days by rental car or bus tour. Cost figures are undated estimates, so check current prices.

Shukubo are temples with lodging for pilgrims. The association advises checking and reserving before you go.

## Dress and the nokyocho

**Clothing.** The temple association's FAQ says there is no required dress, but recommends white garments. It suggests that a white coat (hakui) and collar can be worn over ordinary clothes. The Shikoku tourism body recommends at least a white vest and a staff.

Traditional items listed by the association and the tourism body include:

- **Sugegasa:** a sedge hat for sun, wind and rain.
- **Hakui:** the white robe or vest.
- **Wagesa:** a stole worn when visiting temples.
- **Kongozue:** the walking staff, which the association calls a symbol of the master. The association says not to use it on bridges, and to leave it in the stand at each temple.
- **Juzu:** prayer beads.
- **Zudabukuro:** a bag for the stamp book and sutra book.
- **Osamefuda:** name slips left at each temple. Their colour shows how many circuits you have made: white for 1 to 4, green for 5 to 6, red for 7 to 24, silver for 25 to 49, gold for 50 to 99.

**Nokyocho.** The nokyocho is the pilgrim's book. The association describes it as proof of chanting at the main hall and the Daishi hall of each temple. Each temple's office (nokyosho) writes in it and stamps it. The Shikoku tourism body gives a fee of 300 yen per stamp and opening hours of 7am to 5pm all year, and advises allowing extra time in busy seasons. The association says payment is basically accepted from 7am to 5pm. The association says a colour temple image (omie) is given for 200 yen.

## Etiquette at each temple

The association's worship method is:

1. Bow at the gate before entering.
2. Wash at the water basin: left hand, right hand, then rinse your mouth.
3. Ask whether the bell may be rung. If so, ring it once. The tourism body says it is bad luck to ring it when you leave.
4. At the main hall, light a candle and incense, put a name slip in the box, make an offering and recite the sutras.
5. Repeat this at the Daishi hall.
6. Have your nokyocho stamped at the office.
7. Bow at the gate as you leave.

The association also asks pilgrims to place incense in the centre of the burner and candles at the top, so others have room. It says not to take offerings or name slips from the boxes.

## Getting to Shikoku

The temple association lists the main gateways by prefecture: JR Tokushima Station and Tokushima Awaodori Airport; JR Kochi Station and Kochi Ryoma Airport; JR Matsuyama Station and Matsuyama Airport; JR Takamatsu Station and Takamatsu Airport. By rail from Honshu, JR Shikoku's rapid Marine-Liner links Okayama and Takamatsu in an average of 55 minutes. See the [rail routes table](/rail-routes) and the [regions guide](/guide/regions).

The Shikoku tourism body says the All Shikoku Rail Pass covers railways in Shikoku and that cars can be rented near stations and airports. See [regional rail passes](/articles/regional-rail-passes).

## Before you go

- The route length differs between official sources (about 1,200 km or about 1,400 km).
- Stamp offices keep fixed hours, so plan each day around reaching temples before closing.
- Book shukubo and other lodging ahead.
- The Shikoku tourism body says no foreign-language bus tours are currently offered.
