Japanese trains run on a small set of shared manners, and smoking is restricted both indoors and, in many places, on the street. This article sets out what JR West and the Japan National Tourism Organization (JNTO) ask of passengers, and what the national law and Tokyo authorities say about smoking.

## Phones and noise

JR West asks passengers to "set your cell phone to silent mode and refrain from making/accepting calls while on the train." It also asks people not to speak loudly or shout on board, and not to use smartphones while walking in the station or on the train.

JNTO says the same. Its etiquette guide says talking "in preferably soft voices" on the train is acceptable, but "speaking at any volume on your phone is generally frowned upon while riding trains and buses." JNTO's train guide adds one practical exception: on long-distance trains such as the Shinkansen, "it is okay to use the corridor between cars" for a phone call.

## Priority seats and women-only cars

**Priority seats.** JR West says priority seats are marked with "Priority Seat" stickers. They are for expectant mothers, nursing mothers, people with disabilities, the elderly and other passengers who need them. "If one of these people is in need of the seat, please let them use it." JNTO's guidance is the same: priority seating should be given to elderly, pregnant or disabled riders.

**Women-only cars.** JR West says cars with a "Women Only" sticker are for female passengers only. Male passengers who are no older than elementary school age, and male passengers who need assistance, such as those in nursing care, may also ride. JR West asks passengers to check for the stickers when boarding. JNTO says such cars are often reserved for women during the morning rush hour. Neither page lists which lines or hours apply, so check the signs on the platform and the car.

## Queuing, luggage and backpacks

JR West asks passengers to form lines while waiting for the train and to board in order. It says cutting in line or rushing for the train causes trouble for everyone. JNTO advises lining up to the side of the train door so that passengers can get off before you board. Its responsible travel guide says to check where to line up when waiting.

On luggage, JR West asks that the area around the train doors is not used to store suitcases and other large items, so that others can get on and off. JNTO suggests taking your backpack off and carrying it by hand or putting it on the luggage rack. Its responsible travel guide also describes wearing a backpack on your front as good manners, because it stops you knocking into people.

For sending large bags ahead instead, see [Luggage forwarding with takkyubin](/articles/luggage-forwarding-takkyubin).

## Smoking on trains and in stations

JNTO's train guide says there is "no smoking or vaping" on public transport. On the Tokaido Shinkansen, JR Central's seating page states that "all cars are non-smoking" on each of its train models.

## Indoor smoking: the national law

Japan's revised Health Promotion Act took full effect on 1 April 2020, according to the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare (MHLW). Under it, indoor smoking is in principle banned in many facilities. Smoking indoors is only allowed in designated smoking rooms. The MHLW lists three kinds: smoking-only rooms, rooms for heated tobacco only, and rooms where smoking is permitted. Which kind a business may have depends on its category. Smoking rooms must display a sign.

People under 20 may not enter smoking areas. The MHLW says this applies to both customers and staff.

The Tokyo Metropolitan Government's summary of the national law and its own ordinance says facilities used by two or more people are generally required to be no smoking inside. It says the rules cover heated tobacco as well as cigarettes. Type I facilities, such as hospitals and schools, cannot have indoor smoking rooms. Restaurants must display their smoking policy at the entrance. Tokyo also notes that general izakaya and restaurants do not count as purpose-built smoking facilities. It says violations may lead to guidance from health centres and may also result in fines.

JNTO's local laws page sums up the effect for visitors: "Smoking is not allowed in most restaurants and bars, except in designated areas." Its etiquette guide adds that "some restaurants and coffee shops still allow smoking", so look for the sign at the door.

## Smoking on the street

The Tokyo Metropolitan Government says that where smoking is not prohibited, including outdoors, smokers have an obligation to make sure their second-hand smoke does not affect people nearby. Street smoking bans themselves are set by local ordinances, such as Chiyoda City's below. JNTO's etiquette guide says "smoking while walking on the street or throwing your butts on the ground is prohibited and may incur a fine," and that designated outdoor smoking areas are limited. JNTO's FAQ says smoking, including vaping, is prohibited in many public places, and asks visitors to use designated smoking areas.

**Example: Chiyoda City, Tokyo.** Chiyoda was the first in Japan to ban street smoking with a penalty, according to the ward. Its ordinance was enacted in 2002. The ward's FAQ says roads across the whole ward, except the Imperial Palace, are no-smoking all day. Its English visitor page says violators are fined 2,000 yen. The FAQ gives 2,000 yen as the current fine and up to 20,000 yen as the maximum. Since 1 November 2024, the ward has fined people who use heated tobacco on the street in the same way as cigarette smokers.

This article covers Chiyoda's rules only. Check the rules of the ward or city you are in, and use designated smoking areas.

## Before you go

- Set your phone to silent before boarding, and take calls between cars on long-distance trains.
- Line up at the platform markings and let passengers off first.
- Keep doorways clear of luggage.
- Do not smoke or vape on trains, or while walking on the street. Use designated smoking areas.
- If you are under 20, you may not enter a smoking area in Japan.
- For paying fares with a card, see [IC cards: Suica, PASMO and ICOCA](/articles/ic-cards-suica-pasmo-icoca).
