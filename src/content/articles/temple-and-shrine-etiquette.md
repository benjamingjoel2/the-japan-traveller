Shrines and temples are working places of worship, and visitors are welcome at most of them. This article explains how to tell the two apart and the basic etiquette for each, based on guidance from the Japan National Tourism Organization (JNTO) and the Association of Shinto Shrines (Jinja Honcho).

## Shrines and temples: the difference

JNTO explains the difference this way:

- **Shrines** serve the Shinto religious tradition. They are marked by a torii gate at the entrance. JNTO says every shrine has a torii, even one without a main sanctuary, and that the torii marks the boundary between sacred ground and the everyday world.
- **Temples** serve the Buddhist tradition. They are marked by a sanmon gate at the entrance. JNTO notes that large temple gates, such as those at Todaiji and Sensoji, have guardian statues called niozo.

The Association of Shinto Shrines says a typical torii has two upright pillars and two horizontal beams. Torii are usually wood or stone, and are sometimes painted vermilion. It calls the presence of a torii "a clear indication of a jinja", the Japanese word for a Shinto shrine.

## Entering and purifying

At a shrine, JNTO says to bow once before the torii. Walk through slightly to the left or right of centre, not straight down the middle. The Association of Shinto Shrines also says to pause and bow slightly at the torii.

Near the entrance there is usually a water basin, the temizuya. The Association of Shinto Shrines says to rinse your hands and mouth there before praying, if a font is available. JNTO describes the steps:

1. Hold the ladle in your right hand, scoop water and pour it over your left hand.
2. Switch hands and rinse your right hand.
3. Pour water into your cupped left hand, rinse your mouth and spit beside the fountain.

JNTO says to never touch the ladle directly to your mouth.

## How to pray at a shrine

The Association of Shinto Shrines describes the standard way to pray:

1. Stand up straight, facing the prayer hall or sanctuary.
2. If you wish, put coins in the offering box. If there is a bell, shake the rope to ring it.
3. Bow deeply twice.
4. Stand up straight and bring your hands together in front of your chest.
5. Clap twice.
6. Say a quiet prayer if you wish.
7. Bow deeply once.

JNTO gives the same sequence of two bows, two claps and a final bow. The Association says this standard way of paying respects is acceptable at any shrine in Japan. For visiting a major shrine, see also /articles/ise-jingu.

At larger shrines you pray in front of the prayer hall, called the haiden, with the main sanctuary normally behind it. The Association says you should not enter the prayer hall without permission, and most people pray from outside.

## How to pray at a temple

JNTO says temples are "not as strict as shrines in terms of prayer ritual", but visitors should be calm and respectful. Its guidance:

- Purify at the temizuya first if there is one, then go to the altar.
- There is no clapping at a temple. Pray silently.
- Many temples have incense (osenko), bought by putting a coin in the offering box. Light it, then put out the flame by waving your hand, not by blowing. Place it in the burner and pray.

For the temple routine on the Shikoku pilgrimage, see /articles/shikoku-88-temple-pilgrimage.

## Goshuin stamps

A goshuin is a stamp and handwritten inscription from a shrine or temple. A guest post on JNTO's blog, published in December 2020, describes the custom:

- Staff stamp the page in red and add details in black ink. Each site has its own design.
- The stamps are collected in a book called a goshuincho. If you do not have one, you can receive the goshuin on a loose sheet.
- Visitors should pray before lining up for a stamp.
- Open the book to the page you want and hand it to the staff.
- At busy times, you may be asked to leave your book and collect it later.
- Goshuin are considered sacred, so do not put other kinds of stamps in the book.
- If you cannot find where they are given, look for the counter selling amulets and fortune slips, or ask a member of staff.

The same post says goshuin "usually require a donation of around 300-500 yen". That figure is from 2020 and may have changed.

## Shoes, photography and dress

- **Shoes.** JNTO says you may need to take your shoes off before entering temple buildings. Leave them on the shelves at the entrance, or carry them in the plastic bags some temples provide. JNTO's manners guide says some temples have no-shoe policies, so check first.
- **Socks.** As shoes may come off, JNTO's advice is to wear good socks.
- **Photography.** JNTO says to watch for signs that prohibit photography inside temples.
- **Dress.** The official sources consulted give no dress code beyond socks. Follow any signs at the site.

## Before you go

- Look for the torii (shrine) or the sanmon gate (temple) to know which etiquette to follow.
- Carry some coins for offerings, incense and goshuin donations. See /questions/do-i-need-cash-in-japan.
