Japan's rail network is set up to help wheelchair users, but much of that help works best when it is arranged in advance. This article covers how station staff assist, how to reserve wheelchair spaces on long-distance trains, the size limits the JR companies set, and the official resources for planning.

## What to expect

JNTO's guide to travelling with a disability states that about 95 percent of Japan's train stations are entirely accessible. It says a wheelchair user should have little trouble getting around major train stations, airports and hotels, and most newer shopping centres and city theatres. Pavements are generally smooth, and raised yellow tiles mark safe walking routes for people with visual impairments. Some tourist sites lend wheelchairs.

JNTO's accessible tourism page says railway staff, bus drivers and hotel staff will help, and encourages travellers to ask when they need assistance. On buses, drivers help wheelchair users on and off and make sure the ride is secure.

JNTO also states the main gap plainly: most standard taxis cannot carry passengers in electric wheelchairs. Plan taxi journeys with that in mind.

For hotels, JNTO notes that some offer accessible bathing or showering facilities and an assisted bathing service. Ask the hotel directly before booking.

## Help from station staff

Station staff can guide wheelchair users to the platform and help them on and off the train. JNTO's advice is to tell staff where you want to get off, so that staff at your destination are ready to help you leave the train.

JR West asks passengers to arrange this in advance. Its English accessibility page states that arrangements with station staff need to be made beforehand, and offers a web contact form for requests. JR Central, in its guidance for passengers using mobility scooters on conventional lines, encourages passengers to notify it as soon as the trip is arranged, either at the service counter of the departure station or through its telephone information centre (in Japanese). After that, JR Central arranges staff at the destination station. It also encourages passengers to come to the station early.

## Wheelchair spaces on the Shinkansen and limited express trains

The Japan Rail Pass official site states that wheelchair-accessible seats are available on most Shinkansen and conventional-line limited express trains, and that they are reserved at any JR Ticket Office (Midori-no-madoguchi). Reservations open at 10:00 a.m., up to one month before travel. If the wheelchair seats are full, or for other reasons, you may not be able to board the train you planned to take.

On the Tokaido Shinkansen, JR Central's seating guide marks the wheelchair-accessible seat as requiring a reservation, and states that reservations are accepted until two days before boarding. The same guide describes the multi-purpose room as primarily for passengers with disabilities, and notes that train configuration may change without notice.

JR West gives the same booking window on its English accessibility page: apply no earlier than 10:00 a.m. one month before the travel date, and no later than two days before boarding. It adds that on cars with a deck, wheelchair users need to use the multi-purpose room. If the planned car changes or does not run, the next train with a multi-purpose room is used.

| Point | What the official pages say |
|---|---|
| Where to reserve | JR Ticket Office (Midori-no-madoguchi); JR West also takes requests through a web form |
| Opens | 10:00 a.m., one month before travel |
| Closes | Two days before boarding (JR Central Tokaido Shinkansen; JR West) |
| Boarding guaranteed? | No: if spaces are full you may need a different train |

JR Central's mobility scooter guidance (dated 2018) adds that it will try to accept applications up to the day before or the day of departure, but encourages early booking because you may not be able to board if spaces are full.

## Wheelchair size limits

JR West states that wheelchairs no longer or taller than 120 cm and no wider than 70 cm can be taken onto the train, and that the wheelchair must be able to turn 180 degrees in a passage narrower than 180 cm. JR Central's mobility scooter guidance gives the same 70 cm width and 120 cm length and height, and says these are the same dimensions required for manual and other wheelchairs.

The Japan Rail Pass site notes that wheelchairs must meet conditions on size and other characteristics, and that powered chairs with steering wheels generally are not allowed unless certain conditions are met. JR Central's guidance states that on the Tokaido and Sanyo Shinkansen, mobility scooters can only use the multi-purpose room of Series N700 carriages, and only if they meet specific turning requirements.

JR West and JR Central both ask passengers who cannot use the toilet or move around the train on their own to travel with a companion.

## Planning resources

- **JNTO accessible tourism page**: links to barrier-free information for Tokyo and Yokohama, an MLIT guide to places to eat and drink for older travellers and people with disabilities, and a barrier-free Tokyo itinerary.
- **Ecomo rakuraku** (ecomo-rakuraku.jp/en): named by JNTO for checking the accessibility of stations across Japan and mapping barrier-free routes.
- **Japan Visitor Hotline**: JNTO's visitor helpline, linked from its accessibility pages.

JNTO itself advises independent research for the most current information, so confirm details with each operator before you travel.

## Before you go

- Reserve wheelchair spaces from 10:00 a.m. one month before travel, at a JR Ticket Office or, for JR West, via its web form.
- Check your wheelchair against the 120 cm by 70 cm limits and the turning requirement.
- Tell station staff where you will get off, and arrive at the station early.
- Do not count on hailing a standard taxi with an electric wheelchair.
- For how the rail network and passes work, see [Rail in Japan and the Japan Rail Pass](/guide/rail-and-the-jr-pass).
