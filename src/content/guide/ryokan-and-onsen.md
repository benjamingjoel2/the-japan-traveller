A ryokan is a traditional Japanese inn. Most have tatami-floored rooms, futon bedding laid out in the evening, and a communal or private hot-spring bath. Many include dinner and breakfast in the room rate.

## Arriving and the room

- Take your shoes off at the entrance (genkan). The inn provides slippers for corridors.
- Take slippers off before stepping onto tatami. Walk on tatami in socks or bare feet.
- Separate slippers are usually provided for the toilet; leave them there.
- A yukata (cotton robe) is provided to wear around the inn and to the bath. Wrap the left side over the right.
- Staff lay out futons while you are at dinner or the bath, and put them away in the morning.

## Meals

Dinner at a ryokan is often kaiseki: a set sequence of small seasonal courses. Meal times are usually fixed when you check in, and arriving late can mean missing dinner. Tell the inn about dietary needs and allergies when you book, not on arrival: many dishes use dashi, a fish stock.

## Onsen etiquette

- Wash and rinse your whole body at the shower stations before you get into the bath.
- Bathing is naked. Swimsuits are not worn in most onsen.
- Keep your small towel out of the water. Many people fold it on their head.
- Tie long hair up so it does not touch the water.
- Baths are usually separated by sex, with signs for men (男) and women (女). Inns sometimes swap them between the evening and the morning.
- Drink water and do not stay in too long; the water is hot.

## Tattoos

Policies vary by bath. Some onsen and inns refuse entry to people with tattoos, some allow small tattoos covered with a patch, and some welcome tattoos. Check with the inn before you book. Private baths (kashikiri or in-room baths) avoid the question.

## Booking

Popular ryokan have few rooms, and peak dates (cherry blossom, autumn leaves in Kyoto, Golden Week, Obon and New Year) go first. See [How far ahead should I book a ryokan?](/questions/how-far-ahead-should-i-book-a-ryokan)
