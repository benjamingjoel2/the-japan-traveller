Tipping is not part of everyday service in Japan. This is the position of the Japan National Tourism Organization (JNTO), and it applies to most situations a visitor will meet.

## What JNTO says

JNTO's tipping page says that "tipping practices in Japan differ significantly from those in many other countries". It states that "it's not common to tip for services such as those provided in bars, cafes, restaurants, taxis, and hotels."

The short summary on JNTO's planning page is firmer. It says tipping "is not practiced in Japan" and that "it can cause discomfort and confusion if you do".

## Service charges

JNTO's planning page says that "a service charge is generally added on to the final bill in restaurants". Check your bill to see whether one has been added. JNTO does not say how much the charge usually is, so this answer does not give a figure.

## When a gratuity may fit

JNTO describes a Japanese custom called kokorozuke, meaning a gratuity given "from the heart", in some situations. It gives one example where a tip may be appropriate: a private guide or interpreter who is used to overseas practices may accept one as a gesture of thanks. JNTO adds that "tipping is not expected and should be done discreetly, even in these cases."

## How to give one

If you do decide to give a gratuity, JNTO says it is customary to put it in an envelope rather than hand over loose notes. Small cash envelopes are sold at convenience stores, stationery shops and 100 yen shops.
