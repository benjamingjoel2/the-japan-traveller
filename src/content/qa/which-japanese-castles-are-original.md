Japan has many castles, but most of the keeps you see today are reconstructions. JNTO puts it plainly: "Many of the castles you see in Japan today are in fact, modern replicas." Twelve castles still have their original keep, known in Japanese as genzon tenshu ("surviving keep").

## The five National Treasures

JNTO's twelve-castles article names Himeji, Inuyama, Matsue, Matsumoto and Hikone as the castles whose original main keeps are National Treasures. JNTO's Matsue Castle page describes Matsue as "one of only five classed as a National Treasure."

- **Himeji** (Hyogo) is also a World Heritage site. Himeji City states it was inscribed in December 1993, with Horyu-ji in Nara, as Japan's first World Cultural Heritage sites. See [Himeji Castle](/articles/himeji-castle).
- **Matsumoto** (Nagano) has a group of five connected structures. Its official site states they were designated National Treasures on 20 April 1936. See [Matsumoto Castle and the city](/articles/matsumoto-castle).
- **Inuyama** (Aichi) was privately owned until 2004, according to JNTO.
- **Hikone** (Shiga) and **Matsue** (Shimane) complete the five.

## What "original" means here

JNTO describes the twelve as castles "untouched by war and natural disaster", in contrast to the modern replicas found elsewhere. There is one qualification. JNTO notes that Maruoka's keep was levelled by the Fukui earthquake of 1948 and then reassembled using about 80 per cent of its original materials.

## Planning a visit

The two National Treasure castles covered in detail on this site are easy to reach by rail. Himeji is on the Shinkansen. Matsumoto is reached by limited express from Shinjuku or Nagoya.
